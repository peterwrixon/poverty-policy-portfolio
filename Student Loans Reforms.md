## 1. Introduction and Macroeconomic Context

The financing of higher education in the United Kingdom has reached an unsustainable equilibrium, characterized by severe material deprivation among the student populace, deteriorating institutional finances, and an escalating long-term fiscal burden on the Exchequer. Under the current status quo, domestic undergraduate students in England are subject to Plan 5 loan terms, which mandate income-contingent repayments (ICR) of nine percent on earnings exceeding £25,000, with outstanding balances arbitrarily written off forty years after the statutory repayment due date. While tuition fees have been marginally uplifted to £9,535 for the 2025/26 academic year following prolonged freezes, the associated maintenance support framework has systematically failed to track inflation.

Consequently, significant structural shortfalls exist between the maximum available state support and the actual cost of living, precipitating a documented crisis in student poverty. The statistical reality of this underfunding reveals severe, systemic occurrences of starvation, homelessness, and utility deprivation. Recent national surveys demonstrate that the average maintenance loan falls short of living costs by £500 to £580 per month, covering less than half of a student's basic expenditures. Driven by this massive deficit, up to 18% of students have been forced to use food banks, and over 60% report regularly skipping meals to save money. Housing and utility insecurity are equally pronounced: 61% of students struggle to pay their rent, 45% are left with less than £50 per month after housing costs, and between 8% and 13% have experienced homelessness during their studies. Furthermore, utility deprivation is widespread, with 25% of students actively cutting back on essential heating to balance budgets, and 30% enduring damp living conditions.

The proposed policy framework envisions a radical restructuring of this socioeconomic architecture to address these acute failures. It transitions the system from a uniform, debt-based maintenance model to a hybrid architecture comprising non-repayable, needs-based grants delivered as direct statutory entitlements for energy, water, broadband, and food. This is paired with a revised ICR loan system designed to cover the actual costs of institutional tuition, supplementary maintenance, and statutory housing costs. Crucially, this model proposes pricing tuition based on the true delivery cost of specific courses, mitigated by the Office for Students (OfS) High-Cost Course Allocation and the economic value generated by students during NHS clinical placements, analogous to differential tuition models utilised in the United States. It explicitly links the student loan interest rate strictly to the HM Treasury's Social Time Preference Rate (STPR) of 3.5% to avoid government profiteering. Furthermore, it abolishes the arbitrary forty-year loan write-off in favour of eventual debt recovery from the borrower's estate during probate, ensuring long-term fiscal fairness to the taxpayer.

This report provides an exhaustive systemic feasibility analysis of this proposed architecture. It leverages international human rights jurisprudence and the Minimum Income Standard to quantify human needs. It applies ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288 systems engineering lifecycle principles to design the administrative framework required to integrate the Department for Education (DfE), utility providers, and private landlords, thereby accurately modelling the systemic costs to the taxpayer. Finally, it forecasts the fiscal differentials and distributional impacts of transitioning from the status quo to this proposed entitlement-based model.   

## 2. Normative and Jurisprudential Foundations of Student Entitlements

The transition to a direct entitlement model for utilities and sustenance must be grounded in defensible, empirical metrics of human need, supported by international and domestic human rights law. The premise that the state should directly provide specific volumes of energy, water, and food relies on the recognition that these are indispensable prerequisites for the realization of the right to education and the right to an adequate standard of living.

### 2.1 International Human Rights Law and the Right to a Dignified Life

The foundational justification for the state provision of energy, water, and food is enshrined in the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR), a core human rights treaty to which the United Kingdom is a state party. Article 11 of the ICESCR unequivocally recognises the right of everyone to an adequate standard of living, explicitly including adequate food, clothing, and housing, as well as the continuous improvement of living conditions. Furthermore, the United Nations General Assembly and the Human Rights Council have explicitly recognised the human right to water and sanitation through instruments such as Resolution A/RES/64/292, framing it as legally binding and essential for the full enjoyment of life and all other human rights.   

Within the domestic context of the United Kingdom, while the Human Rights Act 1998 primarily incorporates civil and political rights derived from the European Convention on Human Rights, the jurisprudential interpretation of a dignified life increasingly intersects with socioeconomic provisions. For instance, the negative right to obtain food without state interference traces its lineage back to the 1215 Magna Carta, which stipulated that individuals should not be fined to the extent that they are deprived of their means of living. In modern public policy, a rights-based approach demands that the state holds a positive obligation to mobilise the maximum of its available resources for the progressive realisation of these socioeconomic rights. Translating these abstract rights into actionable, quantifiable public policy requires an objective, consensus-based benchmark. For this purpose, the Minimum Income Standard is universally recognised as the most robust and defensible metric.

### 2.2 The Minimum Income Standard and the Eradication of the Parental Contribution

Developed by the Centre for Research in Social Policy at Loughborough University and continuously supported by the Joseph Rowntree Foundation, the Minimum Income Standard (MIS) sets out what the public agrees is necessary for a minimum socially acceptable standard of living in the United Kingdom today. In 2025, the overarching MIS calculated that a single working-age adult requires £30,500 annually to live with dignity. However, specific research into the student demographic indicates that student needs differ structurally. Adjusting for regional rent prices, a student studying outside London requires £18,600 per year to reach the MIS, while a student in London requires £21,800 per year. Current maintenance loans cover only fifty-five percent of this standard outside London, and sixty-one percent inside London.   

Furthermore, the current funding architecture explicitly relies on an expected "parental contribution" to bridge the gap between these maximum loans and basic living costs. Costing student finance affordability around an expected parental subsidy is fundamentally inequitable and legally incongruous. At eighteen years old, an undergraduate borrower has legally reached the age of majority; their parents hold no ongoing legal duty or obligation to financially support them. Using this unenforceable assumption to balance the state's budget systematically disadvantages students from households unwilling or unable to pay.

The proposed policy explicitly frames maintenance support around the individual as an independent adult. By defining exact statutory entitlements based on the MIS, it provides comprehensive student maintenance support without any inferred expectation for parents to subsidise their children's statutory human needs.

## 3. Quantification of Universal Basic Entitlements

Under the proposed policy, a registered student, whether undertaking a full-time or pro-rata part-time course, receives an individual entitlement to specific volumes of physical resources and digital bandwidth, alongside a monetary allocation for food and housing. This section utilizes current market data and regulatory caps to quantify these entitlements accurately.

### 3.1 Utility Entitlements: Energy, Water, and Broadband (Funded via Grant)

To calculate the individual physical entitlement, the analysis assumes that the average student resides in a four-person shared House in Multiple Occupation (HMO). This necessitates the pro-rata division of standard household consumption metrics into individual allocations.

Energy entitlements encompass both electricity and gas. The Office of Gas and Electricity Markets (Ofgem) regularly updates the Typical Domestic Consumption Values (TDCV) to reflect national usage trends. For 2026, Ofgem established the median household consumption at 2,500 kilowatt-hours (kWh) for electricity and 9,500 kWh for gas. Dividing these household figures by an occupancy factor of four yields the individual student entitlement: **625 kWh of electricity and 2,375 kWh of gas per annum**. Utilizing the Ofgem Q3 2026 price cap rates, which set electricity at £0.261 per kWh alongside a £0.572 daily standing charge, and gas at £0.073 per kWh with a £0.290 daily standing charge, the financial equivalent of this energy entitlement is calculated at **£415 per student per year**.   

Water entitlements are similarly derived from national consumption averages. Data compiled by the Consumer Council for Water and Water UK indicate that the average daily water consumption per person in England is approximately 140 litres. Annualised, this equates to **51 cubic meters of water per student.** Based on average metered billing rates across regional water authorities, the cost of fulfilling this vital resource entitlement is approximately **£155 per year per student**.   

In the contemporary educational environment, broadband internet is not a luxury but a fundamental utility required for distance learning, virtual lectures, and academic research. A minimum acceptable standard for a multi-user household requires sufficient capacity to prevent latency during concurrent high-definition video conferencing. Studies indicate that a household requires at least 1**00 megabits per second (Mbps)** to support this functionality, which, when divided among four students, guarantees a capacity of **25 Mbps per student**. Furthermore, to support the downloading of extensive academic materials and streaming, the entitlement must provide an **unlimited gigabyte (GB) data allowance**, as restricted hotspot plans capped at 100 to 200 GB are insufficient for modern academic demands. Assuming an average mid-tier fibre broadband cost of £30 per month shared across the household, the **annual per-student cost to the state is £90**.

### 3.2 Supermarket Goods Basket and Sustenance (Funded via Grant)

The right to adequate food must translate into a dietary basket that ensures physical and mental faculties are maintained, preventing malnutrition and food insecurity. The 2025 Joseph Rowntree Foundation MIS meticulously budgets £75.60 per week for food for a single working-age adult. Assuming a standard academic year of thirty-nine weeks, the food portion of the entitlement—paid directly as a non-repayable cash grant to the student—amounts to £3,000 per year. This grant bypasses the utility offset mechanism, empowering students with the autonomy to purchase culturally and medically appropriate foodstuffs. It is strongly recommended that this is uprated by CPI each year to maintain affordability of food. 

### 3.3 Housing Cost Entitlements (Funded via ICR Loan)

Housing represents the largest, most inflexible, and most regionally variable expenditure for higher education students. Data from the Office for National Statistics and the HomeLet Rental Index indicate that private stock rents in London average £2,290 per month across all property types. For specific student-appropriate accommodations, single rooms in shared HMOs across the capital range between £750 and £1,250 per month.   

To standardise this highly volatile expenditure, the policy relies on the HEPI Minimum Income Standard housing calculations, establishing a baseline weekly rent requirement of £126 outside London and £191 inside London. Over a thirty-nine-week academic contract, the housing cost entitlement equates to £4,900 per year for students situated outside the capital, and £7,500 per year for those studying within London. Under the new policy objective, this amount is directly transferred to the landlord to guarantee housing security, but it is issued to the student as an Income Contingent Repayment (ICR) loan rather than a grant.

### 3.4 Supplementary Maintenance Loans and NHS Grants

Alongside the tuition and housing loans, a targeted supplementary maintenance loan is retained to cover additional expenditures beyond the basic survival basket provided by the grants. Based on current MIS calculations for clothing and transportation needs, this supplementary loan is fixed at **£2,000 per year nationally**, and **£3,000 per year for students studying in London**. By adjusting this supplementary loan to strictly cover essential extras, the overall scale of student debt upon graduation is tightly controlled. It is recommended that Supplementary Maintenance loan entitlements uprate the loan via a **Composite Transport & Apparel Index**, blending the ONS COICOP Division 03 (Clothing) and COICOP Division 07 (Transport) metrics to reflect actual student purchasing power.

Furthermore, it is important to note that the existing NHS Grants and Bursaries available to Clinical, Medical, Nursing, and Allied Health students will not be amended under this policy. These non-repayable grants (such as the £5,000 minimum training grant under the NHS Learning Support Fund) provide thousands of pounds in additional funding. While separate from the core DfE framework, they should be highlighted as significantly boosting the cost-of-living coverage for healthcare students, further insulating them from poverty during their demanding clinical placement years.

## 4. Engineering the Delivery Architecture via ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288

Delivering this complex matrix of physical entitlements, financial grants, and multi-party reconciliations requires a highly robust administrative architecture. The ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288 standard provides a universal, internationally recognised framework for the lifecycle management of such engineered systems. This standard ensures that hardware, software, human interfaces, and administrative processes function cohesively across the entire lifecycle.   

Applying the 15288 Technical, Project, and Agreement Processes, the implementation of the proposed policy progresses through specific, costed lifecycle stages.

### 4.1 Concept and Requirements Definition Phase & Capital Outlay

The primary technical challenge is establishing a secure data clearinghouse that interfaces the Department for Education student registry with private utility companies and private landlords. During the Stakeholder Requirements Definition process, the system parameters must guarantee that a student's verified enrolment status dynamically triggers their utility offsets and housing payments.

The Information Management Process dictates that a centralised Application Programming Interface (API) gateway must be architected by the DfE. The initial development of this DfE API and the cross-departmental integration with the PRS Database will require a significant upfront investment. Based on contemporary government IT project cost estimates, providing this secure integration is conservatively estimated to require a Capital Departmental Expenditure Limit (CDEL) outlay of £150 million. Operational maintenance, API linkages, and lifecycle updates will subsequently draw an estimated £30 million annually from the Resource Departmental Expenditure Limit (RDEL) budget.   

### 4.2 Utility Offset Mechanism: The Supply Process

Under the proposed model, utility companies are legally mandated to offset the statutory kWh, cubic meter, and GB internet entitlements directly from the household bill. Because students frequently reside in shared HMOs, the billing system must aggregate individual student entitlements at the property level. For example, if four eligible students reside at a single address, the utility provider deducts 2,500 kWh of electricity from the aggregate bill before charging the household for any excess usage.

Following the ISO 15288 Agreement Processes, the utility companies then batch these offset records monthly and submit them to the DfE for direct reimbursement. The government acts as the wholesale acquirer of bulk utility services on behalf of the beneficiaries, potentially negotiating tariffs below the retail price cap.

### 4.3 Housing Loan Integration: PRS Database and DWP Landlord Portal

The administration of the housing loan entitlement leverages two highly complementary existing government infrastructures: the forthcoming Private Rented Sector (PRS) Database and the Department for Work and Pensions Universal Credit Landlord Portal.

The Renters' Rights Act 2025 mandates the creation of the PRS Database, slated for phased rollout in late 2026. Under the proposed systems architecture, a landlord's property must have an active and compliant entry on the PRS Database to receive DfE housing loan disbursements. This verification ensures that state funds are not disbursed to rogue operators or properties failing the Decent Homes Standard.   

Concurrently, the system utilizes the architecture of the DWP Universal Credit Landlord Portal. Landlords who meet PRS Database requirements can register using role-based access controls to confirm the student's tenancy. Once verified, the housing cost entitlement is paid directly from the DfE to the landlord as an advance added to the student's ICR loan balance. This automated routing entirely bypasses the student's personal bank account, eliminating the risk of rent arrears.

## 5. Restructuring Tuition: The Actual Cost Paradigm, NHS Savings, and OfS Subsidies

While fundamental food and utility costs are transitioned to a non-repayable, grant-based entitlement, tuition costs, housing, and supplementary lifestyle expenditures remain structured as repayable Income Contingent Repayment loans. The proposal mandates a shift to an actual cost tuition model, heavily drawing on the United Kingdom's internal Transparent Approach to Costing (TRAC) frameworks, while deducting the economic value students provide to the NHS during clinical placements and applying re-weighted Office for Students (OfS) subsidies.

### 5.1 Critiquing the Flat-Fee Model and the TRAC Methodology

Currently, undergraduate tuition fees in England are capped at £9,535 for the vast majority of courses, regardless of the subject's resource intensity. In contrast, TRAC reporting reveals profound and systemic disparities in delivery costs across subject areas. Under TRAC methodology, the gross cost per student for Dentistry is approximately £32,000 annually, Clinical Medicine is £28,000, Allied Health Professions (AHP) is £13,500, and Nursing is £11,000. Meanwhile, Science, Technology, Engineering and Maths (STEM) subjects cost approximately £12,000, and Arts, Humanities and Social Sciences cost significantly less at roughly £7,000. Under the current flat-fee system, universities are forced into opaque cross-subsidisation, using cheaper courses to fund expensive clinical ones.

### 5.2 Valuing NHS Clinical Placements and Re-weighting the HCCA Subsidy

A critical flaw in the current funding model is that it fails to account for the massive uncompensated labour clinical students provide to the NHS. Students spend up to a third of their degree on clinical placements, directly contributing to patient care and providing immense economic value that saves the NHS from hiring Full-Time Equivalent (FTE) staff. By calculating the FTE replacement value of this labour (factoring in on-costs and a productivity/supervision coefficient), we can derive an 'NHS Placement Saving' which is deducted from the Gross TRAC cost to establish a Net TRAC cost.

- **Nursing:** A nursing student completes 2,300 unpaid clinical hours over three years (767 hours/year). Replaced by a Band 2 Healthcare Assistant (£18.48/hr including on-costs) with a 50% productivity factor (accounting for student status), the annual saving to the NHS is £7,084.
    
- **Allied Health (AHP):** Averaging 1,000 hours over three years (333 hours/year). Replaced by a Band 3 Support Worker (£19.87/hr) with a 50% productivity factor, the annual saving is £3,312.
    
- **Medicine:** Averaging 4,000 hours over five years (800 hours/year). Replaced by a Band 3/F1 performing basic clinical duties (£22.00/hr) with a 40% productivity factor due to high supervision needs, the annual saving is £7,040.
    
- **Dentistry:** Averaging 1,200 hours over five years (240 hours/year of direct patient treatment). Replaced by a Dental Therapist (£33.02/hr) with a 60% productivity factor, the annual saving is £4,755.
    

Once these NHS savings are deducted from the Gross TRAC, the resulting Net TRAC cost is passed to the student as a tuition fee loan. However, the Exchequer mitigates these costs via the Office for Students (OfS) High-Cost Course Allocation (HCCA), which provides approximately £1.25 billion annually.

To aggressively tackle the immense gross costs of Medicine and Dentistry (Group A), this proposed model completely removes the HCCA subsidy from Nursing and strips £1,000 from Allied Health Professions, redirecting these vital funds back into Medicine and especially Dentistry. By leveraging the immense NHS Placement Savings to cushion Nursing costs, the £1.25 billion HCCA pool can be strategically targeted to heavily subsidize dental and medical students, whose gross training costs are otherwise prohibitive.

## 6. Debt Recovery, Probate, Interest Rates, and the Abolition of the Arbitrary Write-Off

The most fiscally transformative components of the proposed model relate to the treatment of the resulting ICR debt. This mechanism resolves the long-term structural deficit of the student loan book by fundamentally altering the timeline of liability and the interest rate charged on borrowing.

### 6.1 The Status Quo: The RAB Charge and the Forty-Year Horizon

Under the prevailing Plan 5 regulations, student loans are written off forty years after the statutory repayment due date. Because repayments are strictly income-contingent (nine percent above £25,000), **a vast proportion of graduates never repay their full balance.** The government calculates this **expected financial loss as the Resource Accounting and Budgeting (RAB) charge**. For the 2025-26 financial year, the **RAB charge for Plan 5 full-time loans** is projected at thirty-three percent (**33%**).

### 6.2 Interest Rates strictly tied to the Social Time Preference Rate (STPR)

Currently, Plan 5 loans accrue interest pegged strictly to the Retail Price Index (RPI). The proposed policy alters this parameter by formally linking the student loan interest rate to HM Treasury's Social Time Preference Rate (STPR).

Crucially, the interest rate is pegged to the **STPR only (3.5% total)**, and **not** inflation plus 3.5%. The STPR (3.5%) is the exact discount rate utilised by the government to calculate the present value of future cash flows. By ensuring the interest rate charged to the student mathematically equals the government's discount rate, the system guarantees perfect Net Present Value (NPV) neutrality on recovered funds. This structure ensures the government recoups the true economic value of the loan over time but absolutely prevents the state from exploiting the loan book for profit margins. While this fixed nominal rate alters the compounding trajectory of the debt, the income-contingent nature of the monthly repayments fully insulates the graduate from un-affordability.

### 6.3 The Mechanics of Estate Recovery at Probate

To ensure this debt is ultimately repaid rather than arbitrarily subsidised, the forty-year write-off date is entirely abolished. Repayment terms during the borrower's lifetime remain strictly as-is. However, any outstanding balance at the time of the borrower's death crystallises into a formal liability upon their estate, recoverable during the standard probate process.

This process mirrors the Department for Work and Pensions' estate recovery protocols in the UK. The Student Loans Company would seamlessly integrate into the existing legal probate process, registering as a primary creditor against the estate before distribution to beneficiaries. **If the estate is insolvent, the debt perishes with the borrower,** averting the intergenerational transmission of negative equity. By treating the loan as a lifelong liability that culminates at probate, the Exchequer drastically reduces the rate of unrecoverable debt while protecting the borrower's month-to-month living standards.

## 7. Comparative Fiscal Forecasting and Budgetary Impacts

### 7.1 Budgetary Treatment and Aligning the RAB/CAB Charge

In UK public sector finances, student loans are subjected to a partitioned accounting approach. Under the European System of Accounts (ESA 2010) national accounting framework, the unrecoverable portion of the loan (the RAB charge) is treated as a capital transfer, directly impacting Public Sector Net Borrowing (PSNB).

However, current departmental budgeting guidance does not align with this national accounting reality. Presently, the impairment charge for student loans scores against the Department for Education's ring-fenced Resource DEL (RDEL).

Under the proposed model, the interest rate is strictly aligned to the STPR (which mirrors capital transfer modelling) and the arbitrary 30-to-40-year write-off rule is removed in favour of a lifetime liability and probate claim.


To map the policy interaction with the core fiscal measures:

- **Public Sector Net Cash Requirement (PSNCR):** Impacted directly by the upfront issuance of cash for loans and grants, minus cash collected via ICR repayments.
    
- **Public Sector Current Budget Deficit (PSCBD):** The non-repayable RDEL grants directly increase the PSCBD, while the expected interest payments received from students decrease it. Because the unrecoverable debt is a capital transfer, it does _not_ hit the PSCBD.
    
- **Public Sector Net Borrowing (PSNB):** The overall headline deficit combines the current deficit (PSCBD) with capital transfers. Therefore, PSNB absorbs the full £5.41B cost of the new grants _plus_ the upfront capital transfer of the unrecoverable loan portion.
    
- **Public Sector Net Financial Liabilities (PSNFL):** Like PSNB, this broader balance sheet measure is only worsened by the unrecoverable charge, because the recoverable portion of the loan remains a state asset.
    

### 7.2 Quantitative Fiscal Modelling

In UK public sector finances, student loans are subjected to a partitioned accounting approach under the European System of Accounts (ESA 2010). Crucially, even if a loan is held until death and the borrower's estate is insolvent, the legal framework of the student loan guarantees that the debt will be cancelled upon death. Eurostat views this predefined cancellation condition as a pre-agreed policy gift rather than an unpredictable volume loss. Consequently, this expected unrecoverable portion is recorded as a Capital Transfer Expenditure (D.99).

To forecast the fiscal divergence across these four ONS aggregates, the analysis utilizes a standardised cohort of 1.5 million active undergraduate students. Because the average supplementary loan is heavily constrained (£2,200 weighted average), the total average graduation debt has fallen to £51,500, which reduces the total annual lending outlay from over £31 Billion down to £26 Billion.

Due to the smaller loan principal, the rate of unrecoverable debt (the CAB charge) plummets significantly under the 9% repayment rate.

The table below scopes the comparative systemic costs if the state selects the **Status Quo**, the **Proposed Model with Probate Recovery**, or the **Proposed Model without Probate** (where loans are lifelong but written off at death). _Note: All models evaluated use a strict 9% repayment rate, a 3.5% STPR interest rate, and apply the £1.25 billion HCCA subsidy._

| Fiscal Aggregate (Annual Impact)      | Status Quo (Plan 5 Baseline) | Proposed Model (With Probate) | Proposed Model (No Probate) |
| ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------: | ----------------------------: | --------------------------: |
| **PSNCR (Net Cash Requirement)**      |                  £10 billion |                   **£11 billion** |                 £17 billion |
| **PSCBD (Current Budget Deficit)**    |                  -£2 billion |                    **£3 billion** |                  £3 billion |
| **PSNB (Overall Net Borrowing)**      |                   £7 billion |                    **£8 billion** |                 £11 billion |

The Status Quo baseline figures were constructed using standard government parameters to provide a steady-state comparison. The 33% RAB charge reflects the government's official projected loss to the Exchequer due to the 40-year write-off for new Plan 5 full-time higher education loans. The £21 Billion lending outlay is a modelled figure calculated by multiplying the standard flat-fee tuition cap and national average maintenance loan uptakes by a standardised, steady-state cohort of 1.5 million active students, ensuring a direct baseline comparison.

By shifting to the **Proposed Model**, the state provides £5 Billion in direct RDEL Grants for utilities and food, while issuing the reduced £26 Billion in new loans. If the state chooses the **No Probate** option (allowing uncollected debts to perish upon death), the CAB charge sits at roughly 21.50%. This drives the total systemic cost (PSNB/PSNFL) to **£11 Billion**. However, implementing the mechanics of estate recovery at **Probate** shrinks the unrecoverable CAB charge down to an incredibly efficient 9.25%. This pulls the true Total Systemic Cost (PSNB/PSNFL) down to just **£8 Billion**.

#### 7.2.1 Uncertainty Bounds and Sensitivity Analysis

The forecast figures for the Proposed Model with Probate carry a moderate-to-high degree of uncertainty. Any financial model projecting income-contingent repayments and estate recovery 40 to 60 years into the future is inherently sensitive to macroeconomic shifts. A probate recovery model introduces additional long-term variables, such as future property market valuations, the frequency of estate insolvencies, and whether end-of-life social care costs might deplete an individual's wealth before death.

Applying a realistic sensitivity variance of +/- 3.5 percentage points to the central Capital Accounting and Budgeting (CAB) charge estimate of 9% yields the following uncertainty bounds:

| Scenario                      | Assumed CAB Charge | Annual Unrecoverable Subsidy | Total Systemic Cost (PSNB/PSNFL) |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------ | ---------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| **Lower Bound (Optimistic)**  | 6%                 | £1 billion                   | £7 billion                       |
| **Central Forecast**          | **9%**                 | **£2 billion**                   | **£8 billion**                       |
| **Upper Bound (Pessimistic)** | 13%                | £3 billion                   | £9 billion                       |

_Note: The Lower Bound assumes higher lifetime wage growth and strong asset retention at probate. The Upper Bound assumes stagnant real wages and a higher rate of insolvent estates at the time of death._ Even at the pessimistic upper bound, the total systemic cost of the Probate model (£8.68 Billion) remains substantially cheaper than the No-Probate option (£10.92 Billion).

### 7.3 The Taxation Case for Probate Recovery vs. "No-Probate"

Selecting the "No Probate" option requires the Exchequer to absorb an additional **£3 billion annually** (£11 billion minus £8 billion) compared to utilising estate recovery.

If this £3 billion shortfall is funded via general taxation, the burden falls directly on the working population. According to HMRC projections for the 2025/26 tax year, there are roughly 30 million basic-rate taxpayers and 8 million higher/additional-rate taxpayers in the UK. If the state raises this shortfall proportionally across income tax, a basic-rate taxpayer would face an average annual tax increase of roughly **£40**, while a higher-rate taxpayer would face an average increase of **£250**.

By contrast, utilising the Probate option requires almost zero immediate tax intervention to cover the difference, as the gap closes dramatically.

This taxation reality powerfully argues the case for the fairness of the probate recovery rule. By back-loading the final taxation point to the estate after the borrower has passed away, the state protects the month-to-month disposable income of the entire working population—many of whom never attended university. It is fundamentally fairer to both the borrower and the general taxpayer to recover educational debts from accumulated, unearned estate wealth rather than inflicting punitive in-life taxation on working citizens.

### 7.4 Household Savings and the Eradication of Student Poverty

While the proposed model is fiscally more demanding for the state's immediate cash flow, its distributional impacts on individual households are profoundly positive, achieving objectives that the current system structurally fails to address.

Under the current system, the government formula explicitly relies on families bridging the gap between the maximum maintenance loan and the reduced loan offered to higher-earning households. This expected "Parental Contribution" ranges from £0 for households earning £25,000 or less, up to an expectation of £5,782 per student, per year, for households earning over £62,410. However, the actual average parental contribution currently sits at just £1,752 annually. Because this contribution fails to bridge the true cost of living, students are left facing a massive deficit approaching £7,000 annually between their loan and actual survival costs.

By directly fulfilling the Minimum Income Standard through statutory grant entitlements to energy, water, internet, and food, and routing housing loans to landlords, the new policy completely eliminates both the student deficit and the oppressive burden of parental contributions.

Crucially, this creates a massive net financial gain for the majority of families, even after accounting for the tax increases required to fund the system.

|Income Bracket|Gross Household Income Range|Expected Parental Contribution (SFE)|Actual Average Parental Contribution|Tax Cost (Probate Option)|Tax Cost (No-Probate Option)|
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|**Basic Rate**|£25,000 to £50,270|£0 to £3,867|£0 to £1,752|£10 to £30|£25 to £70|
|**Higher Rate**|£50,271 to £125,140|£3,867 to £5,782|£1,752 (Capped)|£30 to £160|£80 to £450|

As demonstrated, the tax burden required to fund the proposed model (even under the higher no-probate option) is vastly cheaper than the £1,752 to £5,782 post-tax parental contribution currently demanded to keep a child out of poverty.

Therefore, the proposed model effectively generates significant household savings. It frees up domestic expenditure by replacing concentrated, private post-tax burdens with a highly diffuse and far cheaper distributed tax contribution. Simultaneously, the ICR mechanism strictly protects the graduate's cash flow, ensuring repayments only occur when affordable, while estate recovery ensures that the cost of education acts as a progressive check on intergenerational wealth hoarding, collected fairly at probate.

## 8. Conclusion

Transitioning the United Kingdom's student funding architecture to a needs-based entitlement model represents a highly complex but technically and legally feasible systems engineering endeavor. By defining basic human needs through the Minimum Income Standard, the state can objectively quantify and defend statutory grant entitlements to energy, water, internet, and food.

Implementing this system requires sophisticated digital integration between the Department for Education and the forthcoming Private Rented Sector Database, requiring an estimated £150 million upfront CDEL investment and £30 million ongoing RDEL. Utilizing the existing framework of the DWP Universal Credit Landlord Portal ensures secure housing provision, routing housing ICR loans directly to accommodation providers.

Fiscally, the model proposes shifting volatile, unrecoverable capital subsidies into direct resource grants (RDEL) while reclassifying the unrecoverable debt as a CAB charge under CDEL to strictly align with ONS and ESA 2010 accounting. By structuring housing as a loan, deducting the immense value of NHS clinical placements from healthcare course fees, aligning interest rates to the 3.5% STPR, capping supplementary loans to drastically lower principal debts, and utilizing probate recovery, the system's unrecoverable debt falls from 33% to roughly 9.25%. While this paradigm shift marginally increases the total long-term systemic cost (PSNB) to £7.78 Billion, it averts the need to raise taxes significantly compared to a no-probate route. Ultimately, it decisively resolves the crisis of student poverty, relieves thousands of pounds of pressure from the parental household budget, and ensures that the ultimate cost of education is borne fairly by the estates of the graduates who directly benefited from it.
